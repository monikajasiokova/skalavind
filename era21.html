MAGAZINE ERA21 01/2025

Tsjekkia / Topic: DEPARTMENT STORES

Co-editor of this edition

In the 1920s, architect Leopold Bauer described the department store as a focal point of public life where culture, taste, and education converge. This idea formed the conceptual basis for numerous significant buildings which, in recent decades, have been largely overlooked. Economic interests and consumer demand gradually outweighed the architectural and social qualities of these structures. Although often regarded as public spaces, their private ownership places them outside the domain of public regulation. The result is their physical and functional decline, emptiness, obsolescence, or even demolition.

These often-iconic buildings, frequently located in strategic urban centers, represent invaluable records of past economic prosperity. Yet, only a small number have undergone high-quality renovations that preserve their original function. Today, they face stiff competition from impersonal malls and online retail, both of which lack spontaneity, discovery, and human interaction. Rather than enriching culture, they tend to flatten it.

This calls for asking fundamental questions. Has the post-COVID era demonstrated that people’s relationship to physical retail spaces still holds significant value? Should there be increased collaboration between the private and public sectors through multifunctional spaces serving diverse urban activities? Do these former ”temples of materialism” still hold the potential for revitalization and functional preservation, or are they indeed singing their swan song, their fate already sealed?
